Multivoicedness and Moral Injury

Understanding identity disruption and restoration in first responders, through the many voices that make up a self.

Allison Gao, Konstantinos Xanthios, Pouria Saffaran, Michel Ferrari
Wisdom & Identity Lab, Ontario Institute for Studies in Education (OISE), University of Toronto

Scope. This is a small, exploratory study of three life-story interviews (n = 3). It illustrates patterns in these particular lives; it is not generalizable to all first responders.

Background

What moral injury is

Moral injury is the lasting psychological distress that can follow violating, or witnessing the violation of, deeply held moral values (Cahill et al., 2023). It is not the same as fear-based trauma. Where post-traumatic stress is rooted in danger and threat, moral injury is rooted in conscience — in guilt, shame, betrayal, and the sense that something deeply right has been broken. The concept was first named with Vietnam veterans whose suffering the language of PTSD could not fully reach (Litz et al., 2009; Shay, 1994), and it has since been recognized in many high-stakes settings, including the work of first responders.

First responders are especially exposed. Their work places them, repeatedly, in situations where they must act, fail to act, or witness harm under extraordinary pressure and constraint (Maguen et al., 2025). Exposure to such morally challenging events is common in this profession — in one nationally representative sample, roughly four in ten first responders reported such exposure (Maguen et al., 2025). The outcomes that can follow include guilt, shame, and social withdrawal, alongside anxiety, depression, symptoms of PTSD, and burnout.

A careful distinction matters here. Exposure to a morally difficult event is not the same as carrying a lasting injury from it. Most people exposed do not go on to develop significant moral injury — which is part of why this study treats moral injury as something that happens at the level of identity and meaning, not simply as a reaction to an event. Throughout this work, the idea of ‘failing’ is understood as a perceived moral failure: the painful gap between what a responder felt they should have done and what was actually possible in the moment. It is not a judgment that anyone did their job poorly.

Moral injury is a wound to conscience and self — not merely a reaction to a frightening event.

Theoretical framework

Identity and the dialogical self

Professional identity, for first responders, is built on moral values — fairness, responsibility, altruism, the commitment to help and protect (Carminati & Heliot, 2021; Hodges, 2025). When someone’s actions, or the outcomes they witness, come into conflict with those values, they can experience internal dissonance and a destabilized sense of who they are (Hodges, 2025). This is why moral injury reaches so deep: it is not only about what happened, but about what the person comes to believe about themselves.

To understand this, the study draws on Dialogical Self Theory. The idea is gentle and human: we do not carry a single inner voice, but many, in ongoing conversation. The self is a ‘society’ of relatively autonomous positions — an I-as-helper, an I-as-professional, an I-as-caregiver — each able to speak, agree, question, or disagree with the others (Hermans, 2001). Crucially, the self is social all the way down: the people and institutions in our lives also live inside this inner conversation, shaped through relationships and through being recognized by others (Atkins, 2008; Aveling et al., 2015).

I-as-helper I-as-professional I-as-caregiver

Seen this way, moral injury can be understood as a disruption of that inner conversation. Valued moral voices can be thrown into conflict with newly amplified opposing ones, creating internal tension and a sense of fragmentation. The hopeful side of the same idea is that, because the self is sustained through recognition and relationship, supportive voices can re-enter the conversation and help a coherent sense of self be rebuilt.

We do not have one inner voice. We have many, in conversation — and moral injury is when that conversation falls into conflict.

Methods

How we listened: the multivoicedness method

This study analyzed three life-story interviews with first responders, who took part through the Valhalla Project Niagara, a Canadian non-profit supporting veterans and first responders living with post-traumatic stress through community, connection, and purpose (Arnoldi, 2023). The interviews focused on life experiences, meaning-making, and personal histories rather than on any single incident.

We used a published three-step approach to analyzing multivoicedness (Aveling et al., 2015). First, we noticed the different ‘I-voices’ a person speaks from — the distinct identity positions in their account. Second, we listened for the voices of others living within the account — family, colleagues, institutions — the inner-Others who are part of how a person understands themselves. Third, we examined how all of these voices relate: where they support one another, and where they pull against each other.

Those points of tension have a name in this method: dialogical knots. A knot is a moment where two inner voices pull in opposite directions, often marked by a quiet ‘but.’ It is the shape of internal conflict, never a verdict on the person. We coded each quotation in the full context of the interview rather than lifting fragments, held our interpretations as provisional, compared our coding collaboratively for consistency, and reflected on our own role as listeners. This is exploratory, small-scale work; with three participants, it illustrates patterns in these particular lives rather than making claims about all first responders.

Results

Moral injury as a conflict of voices

Across the three interviews, moral injury appeared as conflict between core identity positions. On one side stood valued voices the participants had built their sense of self upon: I-as-helper, I-as-professional, I-as-caregiver. On the other emerged opposing voices: I-as-perpetuator, I-as-guilty, I-as-self-critical, and I-as-identity-lost.

The reason the helper identity makes these conflicts so sharp is that it sets a high standard for the self. When a person’s whole sense of who they are is built around protecting and helping, almost any outcome involving harm can come to feel like a personal moral failing — even when they acted properly, and even when the constraints of the moment made another outcome impossible (Litz et al., 2009). This is where guilt can quietly harden into shame: a discrete sense of ‘I did something wrong’ can slide into a corrosive verdict of ‘I am wrong.’ In the language of voices, a self-critical voice can grow loud enough to dominate, drowning out the valued voices it speaks against.

The outcomes the participants described — persistent shame, self-blame, a felt sense of responsibility, and a destabilized moral identity — follow from this inner conflict. This supports the study’s central reframing: moral injury is best understood as an identity-based disruption, not solely a reaction to a traumatic event.

Results

Trauma and the loss of meaning

Alongside the conflict of voices, the interviews showed a shift in the kinds of identities the participants could speak from. Meaning-oriented positions — helper, contributor, passionate professional — gave way to disrupted self-states described as hopeless, isolated, and disconnected. People spoke of feeling ‘lost’ or burdened, of a breakdown in their ability to make sense of their experience and hold a coherent sense of self.

hopeless isolated disconnected lost

This can be understood as an erosion of meaning along several dimensions at once. Meaning in life is built from a sense that one’s life is coherent, that it has purpose, and that one matters (Costin & Vignoles, 2020). Moral injury can disturb all three: the self-narrative no longer quite makes sense, valued goals feel foreclosed, and — perhaps most corrosively — the felt sense of mattering withdraws, as a person comes to feel they no longer count. Research suggests mattering is the strongest of these threads, which is part of why recognition by others is so central to recovery.

It is important to hold this gently. Loss of meaning here is the lived experience of someone whose deepest values were strained by an impossible situation. Meaning is not something a person failed to keep; it is something that was disrupted, and that can, with support, be slowly rebuilt.

Figure 1

A four-stage model of moral injury

Drawing these threads together, the study proposes a model of moral injury as a dynamic, ongoing dialogical process — captured in four stages. In Stage 1, core identities encounter conflicting internal voices: the helper, professional, and caregiver positions meet opposing voices of guilt and self-criticism. In Stage 2, these conflicts give rise to shame, guilt, and self-blame, to a loss of meaning, and to social withdrawal — the constriction of self that follows when self-condemning voices dominate.

In Stage 3, restorative voices intervene: peer support, therapy, mindfulness, and community begin to enter the inner conversation. In Stage 4, identity is reconstructed — meaning is rebuilt and a coherent sense of self is gradually restored. Importantly, restoration does not mean erasing the event or silencing the difficult voices. It means introducing supportive voices that can sit alongside them, so the guilty voice can be answered rather than left to rule alone. Because this is an ongoing process rather than a finish line, the model holds both the depth of the injury and the genuine possibility of repair.

Results

Restoration through supportive voices

In the participants’ accounts, recovery was linked to the arrival of restorative voices: community connection, therapy, spirituality and mindfulness, and creative expression. These supported the emergence of new identity positions — I-as-restored-self, I-as-supported, I-as-expressive — and helped the work of reframing trauma, rebuilding meaning, and reclaiming a coherent identity and a sense of agency.

I-as-restored-self I-as-supported I-as-expressive

Each pathway plays a recognizable part. Community and peer connection answer isolation with recognition; hearing another person who has carried similar guilt say ‘me too’ rather than ‘you failed’ can quietly counter the inner critic — the mechanism at the heart of the Valhalla Project’s peer model, whose participants speak of no longer feeling alone. Therapy offers structured, compassionate ways to re-examine self-blame and separate realistic responsibility from what was never within a person’s control; several approaches developed for moral injury work precisely by introducing a compassionate voice into the inner dialogue (Litz et al., 2009). Spirituality, mindfulness, and creative or narrative expression offer ways to hold meaning, to notice harsh self-judgment without being commanded by it, and to give form to what words alone struggle to reach.

We hold this part of the story with honesty and humility. Restoration is not a return to a pre-injury self; it is an integration that carries the experience rather than pretending it never happened. It is gradual, relational, and witnessed — supported with and by others, never the product of willpower alone — and several of these supports, while widely valued, are best understood as complements to professional care rather than guaranteed cures.

Summary

Supportive voices re-form a self without erasing its wound. The self steadies but does not return to who it was; the difficult voice stays and is answered rather than silenced; the seam remains. Healing is not deleting a voice — it is introducing voices that can sit alongside the hard one, so it no longer rules alone.

Discussion

What this suggests

Read together, these three lives point toward a single general implication: moral injury is an identity-based conflict of internal voices, not solely a reaction to traumatic events. That reframing changes where care can be directed.

For intervention, it suggests gently separating personal responsibility from systemic failure — naming honestly what was, and was not, within a person’s control under duress — so that self-blame and shame can be eased and valued identity positions can be reconstructed. This is not excusing anyone or dismissing genuine remorse; it is restoring an accurate, compassionate account of what was actually possible, so that a burden carried alone can be set down.

For organizations, the implication is that what happens after a difficult event matters profoundly. Invalidation — silence, blame, or abandonment — can intensify moral injury, lending external weight to the voices of self-blame. Recognition and support do the opposite: acknowledgment, psychological safety, and trustworthy care can re-enter a person’s inner world as a supportive voice rather than an accusing one, and make the separation of personal from systemic responsibility credible rather than merely reassuring.

Naming the constraint is not making an excuse. It is telling the truth about what was actually possible.

Key takeaway

Key takeaway

For these three participants, moral injury emerged when core identity voices — the helper, the professional, the caregiver — came into conflict with voices of guilt, failure, and self-blame. Healing began when supportive voices were introduced, and when each person could begin to reconstruct a coherent, meaningful sense of self.

This is a small, exploratory study of three lives, not a verdict on first responders as a whole. But within that careful scope, it offers a hopeful way of understanding a painful experience: that the inner conversation which can fall into conflict is the same conversation through which, with recognition and support, a person can be made whole again.
